While Türkiye is home to thousands of highly reputable exporters and manufacturers, international trade always carries risk. 

This guide outlines common warning signs (red flags) that may indicate increased risk when dealing with a Turkish supplier. **Note:** No single warning sign proves fraud, but the presence of multiple red flags should trigger immediate and deep due diligence.

## Identity and Document Inconsistencies

**1. Refusal to Provide Basic Corporate Documents**
Legitimate Turkish companies are accustomed to providing their *Vergi Levhası* (Tax Plate) and *Faaliyet Belgesi* (Activity Certificate) to establish trust. If a supplier claims they "don't need to share this" or "it's confidential," proceed with extreme caution.

**2. Name Mismatches**
The company name on the website, the email signature, the proforma invoice, and the official tax documents should align. Minor translation differences (e.g., "Sanayi" vs "Industry") are normal, but entirely different corporate entities being interchanged is a major red flag.

**3. "Liquidated" Status in Official Registries**
If a check of the MERSİS database or the Trade Registry Gazette shows the company's status as "Tasfiye Halinde" (In Liquidation) or "Terkin" (Struck Off), they are not legally authorized to conduct standard ongoing business.

## Payment Anomalies

**4. Personal Bank Accounts**
You should **never** wire corporate trade funds to an individual's personal bank account. Legitimate Turkish businesses have corporate bank accounts matching their exact registered legal name.

**5. Third-Party or Offshore Accounts**
If a Turkish supplier asks you to send payment to a company in a completely different country (e.g., "Please send the funds to our logistics partner in Dubai"), this significantly increases the risk of fraud or money laundering complications.

**6. Sudden Changes in Payment Instructions**
If you receive a last-minute email stating, "Our bank account is under audit, please send the funds to this new account," this is a classic indicator of Business Email Compromise (BEC) fraud. Always verify such changes via a live phone call to a known, trusted number.

## Communication Anomalies

**7. Generic Email Domains**
While some small workshops still use Gmail or Yahoo, any medium-to-large manufacturer claiming massive export capacity should have a professional corporate email domain.

**8. Evasion of Live Video**
If a supplier claims to own a large factory but constantly makes excuses to avoid a live video call of the production floor, they may be a middleman or a scammer operating from a laptop.

## Address and Operational Flags

**9. The Virtual Heavy-Industry Manufacturer**
If a company claims to manufacture heavy machinery or large volumes of textiles, but their registered address on Google Maps points to a residential apartment or a virtual office plaza in central Istanbul, their claims are highly suspect.

**10. Unrealistic Pricing**
If a supplier offers prices that are vastly below the Turkish market average or global raw material costs, it is often a bait-and-switch tactic or an outright scam. 

## Escalation Threshold

If you encounter these warning signs, **stop the transaction process.** 

Do not proceed until you have engaged a local third-party—such as a Turkish commercial lawyer or an independent inspection agency—to physically verify the entity and draft binding legal safeguards.
